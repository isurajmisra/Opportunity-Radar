# Signal taxonomy: what evidence might create a business hypothesis?

Use this taxonomy to prevent the research from overfitting to fashionable industries.

## 1. Pain signals

- Frequent complaints about the same specific task or failure.
- Workarounds involving spreadsheets, copy/paste, manual calls, repeated data entry or hired help.
- Publicly documented delays, outages, shortages, returns, queues, waste or quality failures.
- Negative reviews that describe a recurring unmet job rather than a one-off dislike.
- Repeated questions from users that current providers do not answer well.
- Staff hiring for repetitive manual operations or expensive compliance processes.

**What this proves:** at most, that a problem is reported and may be meaningful. It does not automatically prove budget or prevalence.

## 2. Spending and willingness-to-pay signals

- Public procurement tenders, contracts, award notices or vendor lists.
- Businesses paying for a current manual solution, consultancy, overtime, replacement process or incumbent product.
- Paid job postings for a task that might be productised (not all jobs are replaceable by software).
- Price increases, premium tiers, paid add-ons, service quotes and marketplace transactions.
- Customers switching vendors or publicly explaining a paid alternative.
- Import spend, landed-cost pressures, parts/supply constraints or paid maintenance needs.

**What this proves:** evidence that money or resources are allocated around a related problem. Carefully distinguish actual spend from advertised list price or vendor revenue claims.

## 3. Market-change signals

- New law, compliance requirement, subsidy or procurement rule.
- Technology capability becomes cheaper or newly available.
- Infrastructure, connectivity, payment rails or distribution changes.
- Demographic/household shift or changing business composition.
- Cost curve, supply-chain or geopolitical change.
- Platform policy/API changes that create risk, work or new dependencies.
- New standard, interoperability requirement or fragmented ecosystem.

**What this proves:** a condition changed. A separate chain of evidence is required to show that a buyer has a problem and will pay.

## 4. Supply and competition signals

- Long lead times, local coverage gaps, fragmented providers, poor service levels.
- Repeated complaints about a particular competitor capability.
- High incumbency but a clear underserved segment or workflow.
- Service areas where providers are capacity-constrained.
- Products that must be localised, repaired, integrated or maintained after sale.

No competition is ambiguous: it can indicate an overlooked opportunity, low demand, poor economics, regulation or hard distribution. Investigate before interpreting.

## 5. Search and attention signals

- Search-interest increases, related queries and seasonality.
- Public discussions and growing question volume.
- App downloads, marketplace search rankings or community growth, if reliably sourced.

These are discovery signals. Attention is not the same as purchase intent, paid conversion or retention. Do not use them alone to advance a candidate.

## 6. Signal record template

For every material signal, capture:

- Signal ID;
- country/region and segment;
- source link, publisher, dates and source tier;
- source type (official statistic, company price/terms, public review, procurement, etc.);
- observed fact or reported behavior (short paraphrase, not a long quote);
- problem/job implied;
- evidence for frequency/severity;
- evidence of existing spend/budget;
- source independence cluster;
- counter-examples or selection bias;
- what the signal does not prove;
- possible falsification question.

## 7. Anti-patterns

Do not promote an idea solely because:
- a technology is trending;
- a country has a large population;
- one news article says the market is “booming”;
- a tool does not appear in a quick search;
- an online community is large;
- people complain but have no willingness or ability to pay;
- an incumbent has bad reviews without checking how many users are satisfied;
- the founder can build it;
- an LLM can generate a plausible pitch.
